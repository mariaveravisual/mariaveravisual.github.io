Case study

Hidden Suffering: Shrimp Welfare

A visual storytelling project translating research on farmed shrimp welfare into a clearer and more accessible narrative, using editorial design, illustration and information hierarchy to make complex findings easier to understand.

Type Independent concept
Role Visual storytelling · Editorial & information design
Focus Research communication · Animal welfare
Year 2026
01 — Comparing suffering

Creating a common frame for very different harms.

The research models each welfare threat across three dimensions: how many shrimp are affected, how long the harm lasts and how severe the pain is. These are translated into a common metric: disabling-equivalent pain.

02 — Mapping the threats

Structuring a complex welfare landscape.

The 18 welfare threats were organised into six groups — water quality, pond habitat, feed management, health, transport and end of production — to make the research easier to scan and compare.

03 — A counterintuitive finding

Bringing chronic harm into focus.

The highest mean estimates come from conditions that affect many shrimp over long periods. Widespread, chronic harms can add up to greater overall suffering than more visible but shorter events.

04 — Scale and uncertainty

Putting individual suffering into perspective.

Across hundreds of billions of farmed shrimp, individual harms can accumulate to an enormous total. At the same time, substantial uncertainty remains, and the evidence does not support confidently ranking most welfare threats.

Independent concept project based on research by Rethink Priorities. Not commissioned by Rethink Priorities.
Source research Rethink Priorities — Quantifying and prioritizing shrimp welfare threats↗

Plastic Waste in Fiji

An editorial redesign translating research on plastic waste, disposal and leakage in Fiji into a clearer and more approachable visual narrative.

Type Independent concept
Role Editorial & information design · Visual storytelling
Focus Research communication · Plastic pollution
Year 2026
01 — Setting the context

Giving the data a sense of place.

The story begins by situating Fiji geographically, socially and economically, giving the reader enough context to understand the scale and conditions behind the data that follows.

02 — Making the methodology readable

Turning a technical process into a visual sequence.

The original research combines household, commercial, tourism and fisheries data with landfill checks and stakeholder interviews. I reorganised that methodology into a simpler sequence: imports, use, disposal and leakage.

03 — Finding the big picture

Revealing the patterns behind the numbers.

The central data story compares how much plastic waste is generated across households, commercial activity, tourism and fisheries, helping the reader see both the overall scale and the differences between sectors.

04 — Looking beyond total volume

Showing why a small share can still matter.

Fisheries contribute only a small share of Fiji’s total plastic waste, but lost fishing gear and consumer plastics can leak directly into the marine environment. That contrast became an important part of the narrative.

05 — Turning research into takeaways

Distilling the study into a few memorable findings.

The final summary distils the research into a small set of memorable figures, connecting total plastic waste, leakage by sector, tourism intensity and fishing gear leakage into one clear conclusion.

Independent concept project based on IUCN’s Plastic Waste Free Islands research. Not commissioned by IUCN.
Source research IUCN — Fiji Plastic Waste Profile↗

Dark Vessels

A visual storytelling project exploring hidden fishing activity and the challenge of making complex marine data easier to understand.

Type Independent concept
Role Visual storytelling · Data interpretation · Editorial design
Focus Research communication · Ocean transparency
Year 2026
01 — The challenge

Framing the visibility gap.

Public vessel tracking only shows part of the picture. This project explores how satellite data can reveal activity that conventional tracking systems may miss.

02 — What is a dark vessel?

Making “dark vessels” understandable.

“Dark” does not automatically mean illegal. It simply means that public AIS data does not show the full picture.

03 — Seeing beyond AIS

Building a layered view of vessel activity.

Rather than treating AIS, optical imagery, SAR and VIIRS as isolated datasets, the narrative shows how each reveals a different layer of vessel activity at sea.

04 — From detection to meaning

Turning satellite detections into a clearer story.

The visual hierarchy moves from what can be tracked to what remains unmatched, helping explain why hidden or unreported activity matters.

05 — Why it matters

Connecting visibility to real-world decisions.

Making vessel activity easier to interpret can support fisheries management, marine protection, compliance and enforcement, as well as research and transparency.

Independent concept project based on Global Fishing Watch research and data. Not commissioned by Global Fishing Watch.
Source research Global Fishing Watch — Dark Vessels↗

PALMYRA ATOLL · PACIFIC OCEAN

The Forest Below

An independent visual science story exploring what it takes for native forests to recover on Palmyra Atoll, and the hidden ecological relationships that may sustain them.

TypeIndependent visual story
RoleVisual storytelling · Editorial design · Research communication
FocusForest restoration · Mycorrhizal fungi · Biodiversity
Year2026
A PERSONAL REFLECTION

A personal reflection

This visual story was inspired by the beauty of the research behind it.

Pisonia grandis trees, Tomentella fungi, seabirds, manta rays, and crabs coexist in a web of interconnected relationships on Palmyra Atoll. They are all part of the same ecosystem, as if they were one single, indivisible being.

As humans, we are part of it too. We are inseparable from it. When we disrupt the harmony of this system, we only distance ourselves from what we truly are, creating chaos and suffering.

To understand this system is to understand life itself, and the natural connections we so often insist on breaking.

I'm deeply grateful to the thousands of people who dedicate their lives to restoring the natural balance, protecting the life, beauty, and truth of this world.

THE STORY · 12 VISUAL PAGES

Where science becomes a story

From the return of Pisonia grandis forests to the influence of Tomentella fungi, seabirds, land crabs and manta rays, this illustrated narrative brings together published research on Palmyra Atoll. Each page follows a different relationship in the island ecosystem, from the forest floor to surrounding reefs.

01 / 12

The hidden life that sustains an island forest

A visual science story about the hidden relationships involved in Palmyra Atoll’s forest restoration.

02 / 12

Native forest recovery on Palmyra Atoll

On Palmyra Atoll in the Pacific, scientists are studying how native Pisonia grandis forests return and why relationships beneath the soil may matter.

03 / 12

How coconut palms changed seabird habitats

Coconut palms replaced much of Palmyra’s native Pisonia forest, reducing suitable habitat for many nesting seabirds and altering connections between land and sea.

04 / 12

Roots and fungi in native forest restoration

Removing coconut palms is only part of the restoration process. Roots and fungi form close partnerships beneath the soil that may influence forest recovery.

05 / 12

The connection between Pisonia grandis and Tomentella

The research describes Tomentella fungi associated with sampled Pisonia grandis roots across 52 trees, suggesting a close connection whose role in recovery deserves attention.

06 / 12

A vital exchange beneath the forest floor

Trees supply fungi with sugars produced by photosynthesis; fungal partners help trees access water and nutrients from the soil.

07 / 12

Seabirds bring ocean nutrients to island soils

Seabirds carry ocean-derived nutrients back to the island. Their guano enriches soils with nitrogen and phosphorus, linking marine life to native forests and fungi.

08 / 12

From forest nutrients to feeding manta rays

Rain can carry seabird-derived nutrients from the forest into coastal waters, supporting plankton and feeding opportunities for manta rays.

09 / 12

Land crabs and the diversity of soil fungi

Researchers found higher fungal diversity in soils from land-crab burrows than in surrounding soils, suggesting that crabs may influence belowground conditions.

10 / 12

Could native fungi support forest restoration?

Tomentella becomes less abundant farther from existing Pisonia trees. Scientists suggest that bringing native fungal partners alongside young trees could be explored as a restoration strategy.

11 / 12

The ecological connections sustaining an island

Seabirds, fungi, land crabs and trees are connected across habitats. Understanding these relationships may open new approaches to restoring fragile island ecosystems.

12 / 12

Reflecting on our connection with nature

The visual story closes with a personal reflection on the connections between living beings and our place within the natural world.

Independent visual storytelling project based on published research and conservation information. Not commissioned by or affiliated with the cited researchers or organisations. Illustrations are AI-assisted artistic interpretations; ecological details are not necessarily to scale.
